Wave packets, phase and group velocity

A pure sine wave is infinitely long and carries no information about when. Anything that arrives at a definite moment is a sum of many wavelengths — and once there are many, they need not travel together.

Two waves are already enough

Take two waves of almost the same wavelength and add them. Section 1.2 permits it, and the result already shows the effect this page is about.

The fast factor is the carrier: the individual crests. The slow factor is the envelope: the swelling and dying of the amplitude, the beats you hear when two nearly-tuned strings are played together. The two travel at different speeds, and which one matters depends on what you are asking about.

Worked example

Two waves with $k_1 = 10$ and $k_2 = 11\unit{rad/m}$, so $\bar k = 10.5$ and $\Delta k = 1$.

In a non-dispersive medium with $\omega = 2k$: $\bar\omega = 21$ and $\Delta\omega = 2$, so the crests move at $21/10.5 = 2\unit{m/s}$ and the envelope at $2/1 = 2\unit{m/s}$. The same — nothing comes apart.

In a medium with $\omega = k^{2}$: $\omega_1 = 100$, $\omega_2 = 121$, so $\bar\omega = 110.5$ and $\Delta\omega = 21$. The crests move at $110.5/10.5 = 10.5\unit{m/s}$ but the envelope at $21/1 = 21\unit{m/s}$ — twice as fast. The crests slide backwards through their own envelope.

Two waves added give a fast carrier inside a slow envelope, and the two need not travel at the same speed.

Building a packet

Two waves give an envelope that repeats forever. To get a single lump — a wave packet — you need a continuous spread of wavenumbers, and section 1.4 already says how they combine:

$$ y(x, t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} F(k)\,e^{i\left(kx - \omega(k)t\right)}\;\dd k $$
(2.6.1)

At $t = 0$ this is exactly the inverse Fourier transform of section 1.4, so $F(k)$ is the transform of the shape you want. Everything section 1.4 proved about widths therefore applies immediately.

That is the uncertainty principle, and the derivation shows what it is and is not. It is not a statement about clumsy measurement. It is the width relation of section 1.4 — a fact about any wave whatsoever — with $p = \hbar k$ substituted in. Anything that behaves like a wave obeys it; a plucked string obeys the version without the $\hbar$.

Worked example

An electron localised to $\Delta x = 1\unit{nm}$.

$\Delta p \ge \hbar/(2\Delta x) = 1.055\times10^{-34}/(2\times10^{-9}) = 5.3\times10^{-26}\unit{kg\,m/s}$.

Dividing by the electron mass, $\Delta v \ge 5.8\times10^{4}\unit{m/s}$ — about $58\unit{km/s}$ of unavoidable spread in speed, just from having been pinned down to a nanometre.

A localised packet must contain a spread of wavenumbers, with $\Delta x\,\Delta k \ge \tfrac12$ — and with $p = \hbar k$ that is the uncertainty principle.

How fast the packet goes

Now the question that matters: how fast does the lump travel? Not, in general, at $\omega/k$. The two-wave calculation suggested $\Delta\omega/\Delta k$; here is the same answer for a continuous packet, and it comes from a Taylor expansion.

The phase velocity $v_{\text{p}} = \omega/k$ is the speed of an individual crest. The group velocity $v_{\text{g}} = \dd\omega/\dd k$ is the speed of the lump — and therefore of the energy, and of any signal you could send. When the two differ, crests appear at one edge of the packet, slide through it, and vanish at the other.

That last line is worth pausing on. If a quantum particle is a wave packet, the thing that moves at the speed you would call "the particle's speed" is the envelope, not the crests. A calculation that used the phase velocity would get the wrong answer by a factor of two.

The crests move at $\omega/k$ and the packet moves at $\dd\omega/\dd k$; it is the second that carries the energy and the information.

Why packets spread out

The derivation above stopped after two terms of the Taylor expansion. The next term is what makes a packet fall apart.

Two things follow. A non-dispersive medium has $\omega'' = 0$ exactly, so $t_{\text{spread}}$ is infinite and a pulse holds its shape forever — which is why a shout arrives as a shout. And a narrow packet spreads faster, since $\Delta k$ is large when $\Delta x$ is small: pinning a packet down tightly is precisely what makes it fall apart quickly.

Worked example

An electron confined to $\Delta x = 1\unit{nm}$. Its dispersion relation is $\omega = \hbar k^{2}/2m$, so $\omega'' = \hbar/m$ — a constant.

From $\Delta x\,\Delta k = \tfrac12$, $\Delta k = 5\times10^{8}\unit{m^{-1}}$.

$t_{\text{spread}} \sim 2m/(\hbar\,\Delta k^{2}) = 2(9.11\times10^{-31})/\left[(1.055\times10^{-34})(2.5\times10^{17})\right] \approx 6.9\times10^{-14}\unit{s}$.

Under a tenth of a picosecond. Confine it to a micrometre instead — a thousand times wider — and the time grows by a million, to about $70\unit{ns}$.

Curvature in $\omega(k)$ makes a packet spread, and a narrower packet spreads sooner because it needs a wider spread of wavenumbers.

Check yourself
  1. Give the group velocity for $\omega = ck$.
    Answer
    $\dd\omega/\dd k = c$, the same as the phase velocity $\omega/k = c$. Non-dispersive: packets keep their shape and travel with the crests.
  2. For $\omega = ak^{2}$, what is $v_{\text{g}}/v_{\text{p}}$?
    Answer
    $v_{\text{g}} = 2ak$ and $v_{\text{p}} = ak$, so the ratio is 2. The envelope outruns its own crests, which appear at the back and disappear at the front.
  3. Why can a single pure sine wave not be used to send a signal?
    Answer
    It has the same amplitude everywhere and for all time, so there is no feature to mark an arrival. Sending anything means switching it on, and that requires a range of wavenumbers — at which point group velocity, not phase velocity, decides when it gets there.
  4. Which velocity carries the energy?
    Answer
    The group velocity. The energy is where the amplitude is, and that is the envelope. Phase velocity can exceed $c$ in some media without violating anything, because no energy or information moves at it.
  5. Confine a particle to $\Delta x = 0.1\unit{nm}$, about the size of an atom. What is the minimum $\Delta p$?
    Answer
    $\Delta p \ge \hbar/(2\Delta x) = 1.055\times10^{-34}/(2\times10^{-10}) = 5.3\times10^{-25}\unit{kg\,m/s}$ — ten times the nanometre answer, and for an electron a speed spread of about $580\unit{km/s}$. This is why atoms have the size they do.
