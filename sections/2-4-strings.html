Waves on strings: reflection and transmission

Where the wave equation comes from, and what happens when a pulse arrives at a place where the string changes. The answer is two coefficients that fall out of two boundary conditions, and every feature of the picture — the flip, the reversal, the stretch — is in them.

Symbols on this page

$\Ten$ is the tension in the string, in newtons. $\mu$ is its mass per unit length, in kilograms per metre. $T$ is not used at all here, and $R$ and $\mathcal{T}$ are the reflection and transmission coefficients — ratios of amplitudes, not of energies.

Where the wave equation comes from

Take a short piece of string between $x$ and $x + \Delta x$. Its mass is $\mu\,\Delta x$. The tension $\Ten$ pulls on each end along the string, and because the string is curved the two pulls do not quite cancel — that leftover is what accelerates the piece sideways.

Tighter string, faster wave; heavier string, slower wave. Notice what is absent: the amplitude. The speed does not depend on how big the pulse is.

Worked example

A string under $\Ten = 80\unit{N}$ with $\mu = 5\unit{g/m} = 0.005\unit{kg/m}$.

$v = \sqrt{80/0.005} = \sqrt{16000} = 126.5\unit{m/s}$.

Quadrupling the tension doubles the speed; quadrupling the mass per metre halves it.

The curvature of the string is what accelerates it, giving the wave equation with $v = \sqrt{\Ten/\mu}$.

A join in the string

Now tie a second string to the first at $x = 0$: same tension, different mass per unit length $\mu_2$. Three facts before any algebra, each with a reason.

So a wave entering a heavier string keeps its frequency, slows down, and its wavelength shrinks. Frequency is set by the source; speed is set by the medium; wavelength is whatever those two force it to be.

At a join the tension and the frequency are the same on both sides; the speed and the wavelength are not.

What the knot insists on

Two conditions hold at the join, and everything else on this page is squeezed out of them.

The displacement is continuous. The string is unbroken, so the left-hand side and the right-hand side of the join are the same piece of string and must have the same $y$. If they did not, the string would have snapped.

Put physically: a kink in a massless point would mean a finite sideways force acting on no mass, and therefore infinite acceleration. Nature declines, so the string arrives at the join and leaves it at the same angle.

The string cannot break and cannot kink, so both the displacement and the slope are continuous at the join.

The two coefficients

Send a pulse of any shape $g$ rightwards along string 1. At the join, part of it carries on and part comes back. Write down all three waves, using the fact from section 2.3 that a rightward wave depends on $t - x/v$ and a leftward one on $t + x/v$:

$$ y_{\text{inc}} = g\!\left(t - \tfrac{x}{v_1}\right), \qquad y_{\text{ref}} = R\,g\!\left(t + \tfrac{x}{v_1}\right), \qquad y_{\text{tr}} = \mathcal{T}\,g\!\left(t - \tfrac{x}{v_2}\right) $$
(2.4.1)

The left-hand string carries the incoming pulse plus the reflected one; the right-hand string carries the transmitted one alone. Now impose the two conditions at $x = 0$.

These are usually written with a quantity called the impedance, $Z = \sqrt{\Ten\mu}$, which measures how hard the string resists being moved sideways.

$$ R = \frac{Z_1 - Z_2}{Z_1 + Z_2}, \qquad \mathcal{T} = \frac{2Z_1}{Z_1 + Z_2}, \qquad Z = \sqrt{\Ten\mu} $$
(2.4.2)

One check is worth doing, because $\mathcal{T}$ can exceed one and that looks alarming. Energy is not amplitude.

Worked example

A pulse goes from a string into one four times as heavy: $\mu_2 = 4\mu_1$, so $Z_2 = 2Z_1$.

$R = (Z_1 - 2Z_1)/(3Z_1) = -1/3$ and $\mathcal{T} = 2Z_1/(3Z_1) = 2/3$.

Check the first condition: $1 + (-1/3) = 2/3$ ✓. Check the energy: $R^{2} + (Z_2/Z_1)\mathcal{T}^{2} = 1/9 + 2 \times 4/9 = 1/9 + 8/9 = 1$ ✓.

The reflected pulse is a third as tall and upside down; the transmitted pulse is two-thirds as tall and the right way up. The speeds are $v_2 = v_1/2$.

Continuity of displacement and of slope give $R = (Z_1-Z_2)/(Z_1+Z_2)$ and $\mathcal{T} = 2Z_1/(Z_1+Z_2)$, whatever shape the pulse had.

Reading the answer

Every feature of what you see at a join is contained in those two formulas.

Light to heavy inverts; heavy to light does not. If $Z_2 \gt Z_1$ then $R$ is negative, so the reflected pulse comes back upside down. If $Z_2 \lt Z_1$ then $R$ is positive and it comes back the right way up. The transmitted pulse is never inverted, since $\mathcal{T} = 2Z_1/(Z_1+Z_2)$ is always positive.

The two limiting cases are ends, not joins. Let $\mu_2 \to \infty$ — the string is tied to a wall. Then $Z_2 \to\infty$, so $R \to -1$ and $\mathcal{T}\to 0$: the pulse comes back inverted and full size, and nothing goes through. That is a fixed end. Let $\mu_2 \to 0$ instead — the string ends in a ring free to slide on a frictionless pole. Then $Z_2 \to 0$, so $R \to +1$ and $\mathcal{T} \to 2$: the pulse comes back upright and the end flicks to twice the amplitude. That is a free end, and the doubled amplitude carries no extra energy because $Z_2$ is zero.

Light to heavy inverts the reflection, the transmission is never inverted, the reflection comes back front-to-back, and the transmitted pulse is scaled in length by $v_2/v_1$.

Check yourself
  1. A pulse passes into a string nine times as heavy. Give $R$ and $\mathcal{T}$.
    Answer
    $Z \propto \sqrt\mu$, so $Z_2 = 3Z_1$. Then $R = (1-3)/(1+3) = -\tfrac12$ and $\mathcal{T} = 2/(1+3) = \tfrac12$. Check: $1 + R = \tfrac12 = \mathcal{T}$ ✓, and $R^{2} + 3\mathcal{T}^{2} = \tfrac14 + \tfrac34 = 1$ ✓.
  2. Why can the frequency not change at the join?
    Answer
    The knot is one point of string with one displacement. Both sides move with it, so both oscillate at the same rate. The wavelength changes instead, because the speed does.
  3. What happens to a pulse at a fixed end, and why is it the $\mu_2\to\infty$ limit?
    Answer
    $R\to-1$, $\mathcal{T}\to 0$: the pulse comes back the same size, inverted, and nothing is transmitted. An infinitely heavy string cannot be moved at all, which is exactly what a wall does.
  4. Why must the slope be continuous at the join?
    Answer
    A discontinuity in slope is a kink, and the sideways force on the knot is $\Ten$ times the difference in slopes. A finite force on a point of zero mass would give infinite acceleration, so the difference must be zero.
  5. At a free end $\mathcal{T} = 2$: the end moves twice as far as the incoming pulse. Where did the extra energy come from?
    Answer
    Nowhere — there is no extra energy. Power is $Z(\partial y/\partial t)^{2}$, and at a free end $Z_2 = 0$, so the transmitted power is zero no matter how large the amplitude. All of the energy comes back in the reflection, which is why $R = +1$.
