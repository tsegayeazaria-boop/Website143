Linear algebra, from the beginning

This section assumes you have never seen a matrix. It starts with arrows on a page and finishes with the statement that a Fourier series and a vector written in coordinates are the same operation — which is the fact that makes the rest of this site work.

Before you start

One warning about letters. On this page $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue, not a wavelength, and $k$ never appears at all. Everywhere else on the site $\lambda$ is a wavelength. Section 2.2, which uses everything here, avoids the collision by writing $\omega^{2}$ where this page would write $\lambda$.

Vectors: two pictures at once

You already use vectors. A force of 5 newtons at 37° above the horizontal is a vector; so is a velocity, an electric field, a displacement. You have drawn them as arrows and you have broken them into components. Linear algebra is what happens when you take both of those pictures seriously at the same time.

The arrow picture: a vector is a length and a direction. It exists before anyone draws axes. When you add two forces tip-to-tail, you are working in this picture.

The list picture: choose two perpendicular directions, call them $\vu e_1$ and $\vu e_2$ (you have been writing them $\hat\imath$ and $\hat\jmath$), and every arrow in the plane is some amount of the first plus some amount of the second. Write those two amounts down and you have the vector's components.

$$ \vb v = v_1 \vu e_1 + v_2 \vu e_2 \qquad\text{written as}\qquad \vb v = \begin{pmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{pmatrix} $$
(1.3.1)

The two pictures are not two kinds of object. They are one object and one description of it. That distinction is worth holding onto, because the components change when you change the axes and the arrow does not.

Adding vectors, in components

In the arrow picture, adding is tip-to-tail. In the list picture, adding is adding the numbers separately. Those had better be the same operation, and they are — for the single reason that $\vu e_1$ and $\vu e_2$ can be collected like any other common factor.

Length comes from the same place. The components are the legs of a right triangle whose hypotenuse is the arrow, so Pythagoras gives $\abs{\vb v} = \sqrt{v_1^{2} + v_2^{2}}$.

The dot product

You have met the dot product as $\vb u \cdot \vb v = \abs{\vb u}\abs{\vb v}\cos\theta$, probably while computing work. In components it is $u_1v_1 + u_2v_2$. Those two are not separate definitions; the second follows from the first and the law of cosines.

Two consequences worth naming now, because both come back later. First, $\vb v \cdot \vb v = \abs{\vb v}^{2}$. Second, $\vb u \cdot \vb v = 0$ exactly when $\cos\theta = 0$: the dot product vanishes precisely for perpendicular vectors. That second fact is the whole of Fourier analysis in disguise, and we will collect on it at the end of this page.

Worked example

Take $\vb u = (3, 1)$ and $\vb v = (-1, 2)$.

$\vb u + \vb v = (3 - 1,\; 1 + 2) = (2, 3)$, and $2\vb u - \vb v = (6 + 1,\; 2 - 2) = (7, 0)$ — which points straight along $\vu e_1$, so the $\vu e_2$ parts have cancelled exactly.

$\abs{\vb u} = \sqrt{9 + 1} = 3.162$ and $\vb u \cdot \vb v = (3)(-1) + (1)(2) = -1$. The dot product is negative, so the angle between them is obtuse: from $\cos\theta = -1/(3.162 \times 2.236) = -0.1414$ we get $\theta = 98.1^\circ$.

A vector is the arrow; its components are what you get after choosing a basis, and they change when the basis does.

A matrix is a function

Here is the idea the rest of this page is built on. A matrix is not a table of numbers that you memorise a rule for multiplying. A matrix is a function — it eats a vector and hands back a vector. The table of numbers is just a compact way of writing down which function it is.

Not every function of vectors gets a matrix. Only the linear ones do, meaning functions $f$ with two properties:

$$ f(\vb u + \vb v) = f(\vb u) + f(\vb v) \qquad\text{and}\qquad f(c\,\vb v) = c\,f(\vb v) $$
(1.3.2)

You have already relied on this. When you compute the electric field of two charges by adding the field of each, you are using the first property. When you say that doubling a charge doubles its field, you are using the second. Rotating every arrow in the plane by 30° is linear. Stretching everything horizontally by a factor of two is linear. Adding a fixed vector to everything is not, because $f(\vb 0)$ has to come out zero.

A linear function is fixed by what it does to two vectors

This is the surprising part, and it is the reason matrices exist. If you tell me where a linear function sends $\vu e_1$ and where it sends $\vu e_2$, I can tell you where it sends every other vector in the plane, without asking you anything else.

So write those two vectors down side by side, as the two columns of a table:

$$ \mat A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}, \qquad f(\vu e_1) = \begin{pmatrix} a \\ c \end{pmatrix}, \qquad f(\vu e_2) = \begin{pmatrix} b \\ d \end{pmatrix} $$
(1.3.3)

The columns of a matrix are the places the basis vectors land. That single sentence is what a matrix means. Everything else — the multiplication rule, the determinant, eigenvectors — is worked out from it.

The multiplication rule, derived

Now the rule you may have been shown as an arbitrary pattern of rows against columns falls out with nothing added.

The top entry is the top row of $\mat A$ dotted with $\vb v$; the bottom entry is the bottom row dotted with $\vb v$. That is the rule — but now it is a consequence rather than a convention, and if you ever forget it you can rebuild it in three lines.

Worked example

Let $\mat A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\vb v = (1, 2)$.

By columns: $\mat A$ sends $\vu e_1$ to $(2, 0)$ and $\vu e_2$ to $(1, 1)$, so $\mat A \vb v = 1 \times (2,0) + 2 \times (1,1) = (2,0) + (2,2) = (4, 2)$.

By rows: top row $(2,1)\cdot(1,2) = 2 + 2 = 4$; bottom row $(0,1)\cdot(1,2) = 0 + 2 = 2$. Same answer, as it must be — the second calculation is the first one, with the additions done in a different order.

A 2×2 matrix is completely described by where it sends $\vu e_1$ and $\vu e_2$; those two landing places are its columns.

Doing one after another

If a matrix is a function, then two matrices in a row is one function after another: first do $\mat A$ to the vector, then do $\mat B$ to whatever came out. Whatever that combined operation is, it is still linear — a sum still goes to a sum, and a scaled input still gives a scaled output — so by the last section it must have a matrix of its own. That matrix is what $\mat B \mat A$ means.

And we already know how to find any matrix: apply the function to $\vu e_1$ and to $\vu e_2$ and write down where they land.

Two things follow immediately. First, the rule is not arbitrary; it is bookkeeping for "do this, then that". Second, order matters, because doing $\mat A$ then $\mat B$ is not generally the same as doing $\mat B$ then $\mat A$. Rotate a vector and then stretch it horizontally, and you get somewhere different from stretching first and rotating after. Nothing has gone wrong; the operations are simply different.

The rotation matrix, from the columns

You can build any matrix you need this way, without memorising it. To rotate the plane anticlockwise by $\theta$, ask where the two basis arrows go. $\vu e_1$ starts on the positive $x$-axis, so after turning by $\theta$ its tip is at $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$. $\vu e_2$ starts pointing up; turning it by $\theta$ puts it at $(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)$. Write those two down as columns:

$$ \mat R(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix} $$
(1.3.4)
Worked example

Two quarter-turns should be a half-turn. With $\theta = 90^\circ$, equation (1.3.4) gives $\mat R = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, and multiplying it by itself: first column $= \mat R (0,1) = (-1, 0)$; second column $= \mat R(-1,0) = (0,-1)$. So $\mat R^{2} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} = -\mat I$, which sends every vector to minus itself. A half-turn.

Order matters: let $\mat S = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$ stretch horizontally. On $\vb v = (1,0)$, rotating first then stretching gives $\mat S \mat R \vb v = \mat S (0,1) = (0,1)$; stretching first then rotating gives $\mat R \mat S \vb v = \mat R(2,0) = (0,2)$. Different answers.

Multiplying matrices is doing one transformation after another, and the order matters because the second one acts on what the first produced.

When do two vectors cover the plane?

A linear combination of $\vb u$ and $\vb v$ is anything you can build as $c_1\vb u + c_2 \vb v$. The set of everything you can reach that way is their span. Two arrows pointing in different directions span the whole plane: every point is reachable. Two arrows along the same line span only that line — no combination of them ever gets you off it.

When the two arrows do span the plane, we call them a basis, and they are linearly independent: neither is a multiple of the other. There is a single number that tells you which case you are in, and it has a geometric meaning worth deriving rather than memorising.

The area of the parallelogram they make

Put the two arrows tail to tail and complete the parallelogram. Its area is zero exactly when the arrows lie along one line. So the area is the test. Here it is, computed by subtracting the pieces outside the parallelogram from the bounding rectangle, for arrows $(a, c)$ and $(b, d)$ in the first quadrant.

That number is called the determinant of the matrix whose columns are the two arrows, written $\det \mat A = ad - bc$. Read geometrically: the determinant is the factor by which the matrix scales areas, since the unit square spanned by $\vu e_1$ and $\vu e_2$ has area 1 and lands on this parallelogram. If the determinant comes out negative, the transformation has also flipped the plane over.

Now the algebraic test, which we will need in a moment for eigenvalues.

Notice what the last line says, because it is the sentence eigenvalues are built on. The determinant vanishing and the matrix flattening some non-zero vector to the origin are the same statement. A matrix that squashes the plane onto a line has to send a whole direction to zero — that is what squashing means.

Worked example

$(2,1)$ and $(1,3)$: $\det = (2)(3) - (1)(1) = 5$. Non-zero, so they are independent, span the plane, and the parallelogram on them has area 5.

$(2,1)$ and $(4,2)$: $\det = (2)(2) - (4)(1) = 0$. The second is exactly twice the first, so they lie on one line, the parallelogram is flat, and $2\,(2,1) - 1\,(4,2) = (0,0)$ is the non-zero combination the derivation promised.

Two vectors form a basis exactly when the parallelogram they span has non-zero area, and a determinant of zero means some non-zero vector is crushed to the origin.

Directions that only stretch

Apply a matrix to a vector and two things generally happen at once: the vector changes length, and it changes direction. The second is what makes matrices hard to picture. So ask a narrower question: are there any directions the matrix leaves alone?

A direction is left alone if the output points along the same line as the input — the arrow may get longer, shorter, or flip end to end, but it does not swing off its line. A vector like that is an eigenvector, and the factor by which it stretches is its eigenvalue $\lambda$:

$$ \mat A \vb v = \lambda \vb v, \qquad \vb v \ne \vb 0 $$
(1.3.5)

The condition $\vb v \ne \vb 0$ matters: the zero vector satisfies the equation for every $\lambda$ and tells you nothing, so it is excluded by hand.

Take $\mat A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2\end{pmatrix}$ and try two particular vectors.

An eigenvector is a direction the matrix leaves alone apart from stretching it, and the eigenvalue is that stretch factor.

Finding them, and why a determinant appears

Guessing $(1,1)$ was luck. Here is the method, and the reason the determinant — a statement about areas — turns up in a question about directions.

That is where the determinant comes from. It is not a trick: asking for a direction that survives is asking for a shifted matrix that crushes something, and crushing is what a zero determinant means. Now expand it.

An ordinary quadratic, solved the ordinary way. Its two roots are the two eigenvalues. The sum of the diagonal entries, $a+d$, is called the trace, and the quadratic says the eigenvalues add up to the trace and multiply to the determinant — a fast check on any answer.

Getting the directions back

With a value of $\lambda$ in hand, put it back into $(\mat A - \lambda\mat I)\vb v = 0$ and read off which direction gets crushed.

Worked example

$\mat A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 1 & 3\end{pmatrix}$. Trace $= 7$, determinant $= 12 - 2 = 10$, so $\lambda^{2} - 7\lambda + 10 = 0$ and $\lambda = 5$ or $\lambda = 2$. Check: $5 + 2 = 7$ and $5 \times 2 = 10$. ✓

For $\lambda = 5$: $\begin{pmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 1 & -2\end{pmatrix}\vb v = 0$ gives $v_1 = 2v_2$, so $\vb v = (2,1)$. For $\lambda = 2$: $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 1 & 1\end{pmatrix}\vb v = 0$ gives $v_1 = -v_2$, so $\vb v = (1,-1)$.

Check one: $\mat A (2,1) = (8+2,\; 2+3) = (10, 5) = 5(2,1)$. ✓ And note that $(2,1)\cdot(1,-1) = 1 \neq 0$ — these eigenvectors are not perpendicular. The next subsection explains which matrices guarantee that they are.

Eigenvalues are the values of $\lambda$ that make $\mat A - \lambda\mat I$ squash the plane flat, which is why they are the roots of $\det(\mat A - \lambda \mat I) = 0$.

Symmetric matrices, and why physics is full of them

A matrix is symmetric if $b = c$: the entry in row 1 column 2 equals the entry in row 2 column 1. Symmetric matrices behave far better than general ones, in two specific ways that section 2.2 depends on completely.

Their eigenvalues are always real

A general quadratic can have complex roots — a rotation matrix does, which is right, since a rotation leaves no direction alone. For a symmetric matrix that can never happen, and the reason is visible in the discriminant.

Their eigenvectors are always perpendicular

This one takes a small observation first: for a symmetric matrix you can move the matrix from one side of a dot product to the other without changing the answer.

Now let $\vb u$ and $\vb v$ be eigenvectors with different eigenvalues, and compute the same quantity two ways.

Why physics keeps producing symmetric matrices

This is not a coincidence, and it is worth seeing once in a physical setting rather than taken on trust. Consider two masses joined by springs, the system of section 2.2. Let $K_{12}$ be the force on mass 1 per unit displacement of mass 2, and $K_{21}$ the force on mass 2 per unit displacement of mass 1. Symmetry of the matrix is the claim $K_{12} = K_{21}$.

The spring joining them is stretched by the difference $x_2 - x_1$, and Hooke's law says it pulls with a force $\kappa(x_2 - x_1)$ on mass 1. By Newton's third law it pulls on mass 2 with exactly the same size of force, in the opposite direction: $-\kappa(x_2-x_1)$. Differentiate: moving mass 2 by one unit changes the force on mass 1 by $\kappa$, and moving mass 1 by one unit changes the force on mass 2 by $\kappa$. The same number both ways, because a single spring is doing both jobs and the third law forces its two ends to agree.

So the stiffness matrix of any system of masses and springs is symmetric, its eigenvalues are therefore real — which is essential, since they turn out to be squared frequencies and an imaginary frequency would mean the system flies apart — and its eigenvectors are perpendicular, which is why the normal modes of a coupled system are independent of one another.

A symmetric matrix always has real eigenvalues and perpendicular eigenvectors, and Newton's third law makes the matrices of spring systems symmetric.

Working in the eigenvector basis

Nothing forces you to use $\vu e_1$ and $\vu e_2$. Any two independent vectors will do as a basis, and some choices make a problem far easier than others. For a given matrix, the easiest possible choice is its own eigenvectors — because in that basis it does nothing but stretch each axis.

Collect the two eigenvectors as the columns of a matrix $\mat P$. Then:

Read the three factors right to left, which is the order they act in. $\mat P$ takes a list of coordinates in the eigenvector basis and builds the actual arrow. $\mat A$ acts on that arrow. $\mat P^{-1}$ reads the result back as eigenvector coordinates. The claim is that the round trip is just two independent multiplications — the first coordinate times $\lambda_1$, the second times $\lambda_2$. In its own eigenvector basis, a matrix is nothing but a list of stretch factors.

For $2\times2$ the inverse is short enough to derive on the spot.

Worked example

With $\mat A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2\end{pmatrix}$ the eigenvectors were $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$, so $\mat P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1\end{pmatrix}$ and $\det \mat P = -2$. The formula gives $\mat P^{-1} = -\tfrac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac12 & \tfrac12 \\ \tfrac12 & -\tfrac12\end{pmatrix}$.

Now multiply out, right to left. $\mat A \mat P$ has columns $\mat A(1,1) = (3,3)$ and $\mat A(1,-1) = (1,-1)$, so $\mat A \mat P = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 3 & -1\end{pmatrix}$. Then $\mat P^{-1}(\mat A \mat P)$ has first column $\begin{pmatrix} \tfrac12 & \tfrac12 \\ \tfrac12 & -\tfrac12\end{pmatrix}(3,3) = (3, 0)$ and second column $\begin{pmatrix} \tfrac12 & \tfrac12 \\ \tfrac12 & -\tfrac12\end{pmatrix} (1,-1) = (0, 1)$.

So $\mat P^{-1}\mat A \mat P = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$ — the two eigenvalues on the diagonal and nothing else, exactly as the derivation said.

In the basis of its own eigenvectors a matrix is a list of stretch factors: $\mat P^{-1}\mat A\mat P = \mat D$.

Functions as vectors

Everything so far has been about arrows in a plane. This subsection is the payoff: the same machinery applies to functions, and once you see how, Fourier series stop being a formula to memorise.

Start with the connection. Sample a function $f(x)$ at $N$ evenly spaced points and write the values down as a list. That list is a vector with $N$ components. Sample more finely and you get a longer list; a function is what you get in the limit — a vector with a component for every $x$. Adding functions adds them value by value, and multiplying a function by a number multiplies every value, so functions satisfy (1.3.2) exactly as arrows do.

Then what is the dot product of two functions? Take the dot product of the two sampled lists and see what happens as the sampling gets finer.

So an integral of a product is a dot product. Everything the dot product meant for arrows now means the same thing for functions: $\inner{f}{f}$ is a length squared, and — the important one — two functions are perpendicular when the integral of their product is zero.

The sines and cosines are perpendicular to each other

Now compute those integrals. The tool is the product-to-sum identity, which comes straight from the angle-addition formulas: adding $\cos(A-B) = \cos A\cos B + \sin A \sin B$ to $\cos(A+B) = \cos A \cos B - \sin A \sin B$ and rearranging gives $\sin A \sin B = \tfrac12[\cos(A-B) - \cos(A+B)]$.

The identical calculation with $\cos A\cos B = \tfrac12[\cos(A-B) + \cos(A+B)]$ gives the same answer for cosines. And sines against cosines vanish always, including when $n = m$:

Fourier coefficients are components

Now the question that started all this. Suppose a function can be written as a sum of sines. How do you find how much of each one is in there?

For an arrow you would ask the same question — how much $\vu e_1$ is in $\vb v$? — and you would answer it by taking a dot product with $\vu e_1$, because every other basis vector contributes nothing. Do the same thing to the function.

That is the Fourier coefficient formula, and it is not a formula at all — it is "component along a basis vector", written for functions. Section 1.4 takes it from here.

Worked example

Let $f(x) = 3\sin x - \sin 3x$. What is $b_2$?

Without the orthogonality result you would have to integrate $\int_0^{2\pi}(3\sin x - \sin 3x)\sin 2x\,\dd x$ term by term. With it, you can read the answer off: $\sin 2x$ is perpendicular to both $\sin x$ and $\sin 3x$, so $b_2 = 0$. Likewise $b_1 = 3$ and $b_3 = -1$, and every other coefficient is zero.

The function was already written in the basis. Finding its components was nothing but reading them.

Integrating a product is a dot product for functions, the sines and cosines are mutually perpendicular, and so a Fourier coefficient is a component exactly as $v_1$ is.

Where this is going: a look ahead

This is motivation, not a lesson in quantum mechanics; it is here because the effort you have just spent buys more than coupled springs.

Quantum mechanics is written in exactly this language. A state is a vector — usually with complex components and infinitely many of them, but a vector, obeying (1.3.2). A measurable quantity is a matrix acting on those vectors, and it is always the complex version of a symmetric matrix, for the reason you would now expect: the results of a measurement have to be real numbers, and real eigenvalues are what symmetric matrices guarantee.

The possible outcomes of a measurement are the eigenvalues. The states that give one outcome with certainty are the eigenvectors. Because those eigenvectors are perpendicular, a general state can be broken into components along them — and the size of each component squared is the probability of that outcome. That decomposition is the same operation as writing an arrow in a basis, and as finding a Fourier coefficient.

Even the equations look the same. Here is the normal-mode condition you will derive in section 2.2, beside the time-independent Schrödinger equation:

$$ \mat K \vb a = \omega^{2}\mat M \vb a \qquad\qquad \hat H \psi = E \psi $$
(1.3.6)

Both are $\mat A \vb v = \lambda \vb v$. On the left the eigenvalue is a squared frequency and the eigenvector is a pattern of motion; on the right the eigenvalue is an energy and the eigenvector is a state. The mathematics does not know the difference, which is why doing it once was worth it.

One last piece. If a matrix is already diagonal, the equations it produces come apart into separate, one-variable problems — and you can solve those.

Section 2.2 does precisely this to two masses on three springs, where the $q_n$ are called normal coordinates and the $\lambda_n$ are minus the squared frequencies. Quantum mechanics does precisely this to the Schrödinger equation, where the $\lambda_n$ are $-iE_n/\hbar$ and the result is that a state of definite energy just rotates its phase. Same derivation, twice.

Changing to the eigenvector basis turns a tangle of coupled equations into separate one-variable problems, which is the only reason any of this is worth doing.

Check yourself
  1. Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1\end{pmatrix}$, and check that the eigenvectors come out perpendicular.
    Answer

    Trace 2, determinant $1 - 4 = -3$, so $\lambda^{2} - 2\lambda - 3 = 0$ and $\lambda = 3$ or $\lambda = -1$.

    For $\lambda = 3$: $\begin{pmatrix} -2 & 2 \\ 2 & -2\end{pmatrix}\vb v = 0$ gives $v_2 = v_1$, so $(1,1)$. For $\lambda = -1$: $v_2 = -v_1$, so $(1,-1)$.

    $(1,1)\cdot(1,-1) = 0$. The matrix is symmetric, so this was guaranteed.

  2. Where does $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$ send $\vu e_1$ and $\vu e_2$? Why does it have no real eigenvectors?
    Answer

    Reading the columns: $\vu e_1 \to (0,1)$ and $\vu e_2 \to (-1,0)$. Both basis arrows have turned a quarter turn anticlockwise, so the matrix rotates the whole plane by $90^\circ$.

    A rotation by a quarter turn leaves no direction pointing where it started, so there can be no real eigenvector. The algebra agrees: trace 0, determinant 1, so $\lambda^{2} + 1 = 0$, whose roots are $\pm i$.

  3. $\mat A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 1 & 2\end{pmatrix}$ has determinant zero. Find a non-zero $\vb v$ with $\mat A\vb v = \vb 0$, and describe what the matrix does to the plane.
    Answer

    The rows both say $3v_1 + 6v_2 = 0$ up to a factor, so $v_1 = -2v_2$: take $\vb v = (2,-1)$. Check: $\mat A(2,-1) = (6-6,\; 2-2) = (0,0)$. ✓

    The two columns $(3,1)$ and $(6,2)$ lie along one line, so everything in the plane lands on that line: the matrix flattens the plane onto the line through $(3,1)$, and the whole direction $(2,-1)$ is crushed to the origin.

  4. Without integrating anything, give $\int_{0}^{2\pi}\sin 2x\,\sin 3x\;\dd x$ and $\int_{0}^{2\pi}\sin^{2} 3x\;\dd x$.
    Answer

    $0$ and $\pi$. Different harmonics are perpendicular; a harmonic with itself gives $\pi$, which is its length squared in this inner product.

  5. A symmetric matrix has $a = 1$, $d = 5$ and $b = c = 2$. Show its eigenvalues are real without solving for them, then find them.
    Answer

    The discriminant is $(a-d)^{2} + 4b^{2} = 16 + 16 = 32$, which is positive because it is a sum of two squares — that is the general argument, not a special case.

    Trace 6, determinant $5 - 4 = 1$, so $\lambda^{2} - 6\lambda + 1 = 0$ and $\lambda = 3 \pm 2\sqrt{2}$, that is $5.828$ and $0.172$. Their sum is 6 and their product is 1. ✓
