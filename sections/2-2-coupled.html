Coupled oscillators and normal modes

Two masses joined by springs. Each one pulls on the other, so neither moves in a simple way — and yet there are exactly two motions in which both move at a single shared frequency. Finding them is an eigenvalue problem, and it is the same eigenvalue problem you solved by hand in section 1.3.

You will need

Section 1.2 for the exponential guess and the counting of constants, section 1.3 for matrices and eigenvectors, and section 2.1 for the single oscillator. On this page $k$ is a spring constant, not a wavenumber, and the eigenvalues are written $\omega^{2}$ rather than $\lambda$.

Two masses, three springs

Two equal masses $m$ sit on a frictionless line. Each is tied to a wall by a spring of stiffness $k$, and they are tied to each other by a third spring of stiffness $\kappa$. Let $x_1$ and $x_2$ be the displacements of the two masses from where they sit at rest, both measured positive to the right.

Measuring from the rest position matters: it means every spring is at its natural length when $x_1 = x_2 = 0$, so we never have to carry the equilibrium stretches around. Whatever forces the springs exert in equilibrium already cancel.

The force on the left-hand mass

Two springs touch mass 1. The wall spring is stretched by $x_1$, so by Hooke's law it pulls back with $-kx_1$ — the minus sign because the force opposes the displacement. The middle spring is the one to be careful with.

Read the answer: mass 1 is held by a total stiffness $k+\kappa$ of its own — both its springs resist it moving — and it is pulled towards wherever mass 2 has gone, with strength $\kappa$.

The force on the right-hand mass

Now Newton's third law does the work. The middle spring pulls the two masses towards each other with forces that are equal and opposite, so whatever it does to mass 1, it does the negative of to mass 2.

The two force laws are mirror images, as they must be: the apparatus looks the same from either end.

The equations of motion

$$ m\ddot x_1 = -(k+\kappa)x_1 + \kappa x_2 $$
(2.2.1)
$$ m\ddot x_2 = \kappa x_1 - (k+\kappa)x_2 $$
(2.2.2)
Worked example

Take $m = 0.5\unit{kg}$, $k = 10\unit{N/m}$, $\kappa = 5\unit{N/m}$. At an instant when $x_1 = +0.02\unit{m}$ and $x_2 = -0.01\unit{m}$:

The middle spring is compressed by $x_2 - x_1 = -0.03\unit{m}$, so it pushes the masses apart: on mass 1 it acts with $\kappa(x_2-x_1) = 5 \times (-0.03) = -0.15\unit{N}$, to the left. On mass 2 it acts with $+0.15\unit{N}$, to the right. Equal and opposite. ✓

Totals: $F_1 = -(15)(0.02) + (5)(-0.01) = -0.30 - 0.05 = -0.35\unit{N}$ and $F_2 = (5)(0.02) - (15)(-0.01) = 0.10 + 0.15 = +0.25\unit{N}$.

So $\ddot x_1 = -0.70\unit{m/s^2}$ and $\ddot x_2 = +0.50\unit{m/s^2}$: at this instant the masses are accelerating towards each other.

Each mass feels its own wall spring plus the middle spring, whose force depends only on how far apart the two masses have moved.

The same equations, as one

Stack the two displacements into a single vector $\vb x = (x_1, x_2)$. Both equations of motion are then one equation between vectors, and everything section 1.3 proved becomes available.

$$ \mat M \ddot{\vb x} = -\mat K \vb x, \qquad \mat M = \begin{pmatrix} m & 0 \\ 0 & m \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mat K = \begin{pmatrix} k+\kappa & -\kappa \\ -\kappa & k+\kappa \end{pmatrix} $$
(2.2.3)

Every entry of $\mat K$ has a physical meaning, and it is worth naming them one at a time rather than treating the matrix as a block of symbols.

EntryValueWhat it is
$K_{11}$$k + \kappa$ the restoring force on mass 1, per unit of its own displacement — both of its springs push back
$K_{12}$$-\kappa$ the force on mass 1 per unit displacement of mass 2; negative in this convention because moving mass 2 to the right pulls mass 1 right, which reduces the restoring force
$K_{21}$$-\kappa$ the force on mass 2 per unit displacement of mass 1 — the same number
$K_{22}$$k + \kappa$ the mirror image of $K_{11}$

$K_{12} = K_{21}$ is not an accident of this arrangement. One spring joins the two masses, and Newton's third law forces its two ends to act with the same strength, so the influence of mass 2 on mass 1 must equal the influence of mass 1 on mass 2. That is the physical content of $\mat K$ being symmetric, and section 1.3 already told us what symmetry buys: real eigenvalues, and perpendicular eigenvectors. Hold onto both — the first will turn out to mean the system oscillates rather than flying apart, and the second will mean the two motions we find do not interfere with each other.

Worked example

With $m = 0.5\unit{kg}$, $k = 10\unit{N/m}$ and $\kappa = 5\unit{N/m}$:

$\mat K = \begin{pmatrix} 15 & -5 \\ -5 & 15\end{pmatrix} \unit{N/m}$ and $\mat M = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.5\end{pmatrix}\unit{kg}$.

Check it reproduces the numbers from before: with $\vb x = (0.02, -0.01)$, $-\mat K\vb x = -(15 \times 0.02 - 5\times(-0.01),\; -5\times 0.02 + 15\times(-0.01)) = -(0.35, -0.25) = (-0.35, 0.25)\unit{N}$. Same forces. ✓

$\mat K$ is symmetric because a single spring joins the two masses and Newton's third law makes its two ends act equally.

Looking for a shared frequency

The difficulty is that the two equations are tangled. Here is the move that unties them, and it is a guess — a guess that we then check, which is a perfectly respectable way to solve a differential equation, as section 1.2 argued.

Guess that there are special motions in which both masses oscillate at the same frequency, in step, keeping a fixed ratio of amplitudes. Such a motion is called a normal mode. In symbols: $\vb x(t) = \vb a\, e^{i\omega t}$, where $\vb a$ is a fixed vector of amplitudes and the single number $\omega$ is shared by both masses. As in section 1.2 we work with the complex exponential and take the real part at the end; the equations have real coefficients, so that is allowed.

$$ \left(\mat K - \omega^{2}\mat M\right)\vb a = \vb 0 $$
(2.2.4)

Stop and look at what happened. We started with two coupled differential equations in time, and we now have an equation with no derivatives in it at all. The guess converted calculus into algebra — and into algebra of a shape you have already met.

So the physical question — which motions keep their shape? — and the mathematical question — which directions does this matrix only stretch? — are the same question. The allowed frequencies are the eigenvalues; the shapes are the eigenvectors.

Solving it now needs nothing new. Equation (2.2.4) says the matrix $\mat K - \omega^{2}\mat M$ sends the non-zero vector $\vb a$ to zero, and section 1.3 proved a matrix does that exactly when its determinant vanishes.

$$ \omega_1 = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}, \qquad \omega_2 = \sqrt{\frac{k + 2\kappa}{m}} $$
(2.2.5)

Both are real, because $k$, $\kappa$ and $m$ are positive — and that is the promise of symmetry being kept. Had a squared frequency come out negative, $\omega$ would have been imaginary and $e^{i\omega t}$ a growing exponential: the system would run away instead of oscillating. Symmetry is what rules that out.

Worked example

With $m = 0.5\unit{kg}$, $k = 10\unit{N/m}$, $\kappa = 5\unit{N/m}$:

$\omega_1 = \sqrt{10/0.5} = \sqrt{20} = 4.472\unit{rad/s}$, so $f_1 = 0.712\unit{Hz}$ and $T_1 = 1.405\unit{s}$.

$\omega_2 = \sqrt{(10 + 10)/0.5} = \sqrt{40} = 6.325\unit{rad/s}$, so $f_2 = 1.007\unit{Hz}$ and $T_2 = 0.993\unit{s}$.

The second is faster, which it has to be: more stiffness for the same mass.

Asking for a motion in which both masses share one frequency turns the differential equations into the eigenvalue problem $(\mat K - \omega^{2}\mat M)\vb a = \vb 0$, whose eigenvalues are the allowed squared frequencies.

What the two motions actually look like

We have the frequencies. Now the shapes: put each $\omega^{2}$ back into (2.2.4) and see which vector gets sent to zero.

Those two vectors are perpendicular — $(1,1)\cdot(1,-1) = 1 - 1 = 0$ — which section 1.3 guaranteed the moment we noticed $\mat K$ was symmetric. It was not luck.

Checking both against the physics directly

Neither frequency needs the matrix at all once you know the shape, and confirming them by elementary means is worth the two minutes.

This also explains the ordering. In the in-phase mode the middle spring is idle, so the system is as soft as a single mass on a single wall spring. In the out-of-phase mode the middle spring is worked hardest — stretched by twice the displacement — so the system is stiffer and rings faster. And if $\kappa \to 0$ the two frequencies collapse to the same value, as they should: with no middle spring there is nothing to couple.

The two normal modes are the masses moving together at $\omega_1 = \sqrt{k/m}$, where the middle spring never stretches, and moving oppositely at $\omega_2 = \sqrt{(k+2\kappa)/m}$, where it is stretched twice as hard.

Any motion at all

A normal mode is a special motion. What if you start the system some other way — pull one mass aside and let go, say? Then the motion is a sum of the two modes, and the reason is the superposition principle of section 1.2: the equations are linear, so any sum of solutions is a solution.

Each mode contributes an amplitude and a phase, or equivalently a cosine part and a sine part, which is two constants each:

Four constants, and exactly four numbers needed to start the system: two positions and two velocities. That is the count section 1.2 predicted — two per second-order equation, and there are two equations. The books balance.

What one mass, pulled aside, actually does

So the energy does not stay where you put it. Mass 1 starts with all of it, hands it over to mass 2, and gets it back, over and over. The envelope $\cos(\Delta\omega\, t/2)$ first reaches zero when $\Delta\omega\, t/2 = \pi/2$, so the handover is complete after a time $\pi/\Delta\omega$, and the full round trip — the beat period — takes $2\pi/\Delta\omega$.

Worked example

With the same numbers, $\omega_1 = 4.472$ and $\omega_2 = 6.325\unit{rad/s}$, so $\Delta\omega = 1.853\unit{rad/s}$.

Beat period $= 2\pi/1.853 = 3.39\unit{s}$, and mass 1 first comes completely to rest — with all the energy in mass 2 — after half of that, $1.70\unit{s}$.

The carrier frequency is $\bar\omega = (4.472+6.325)/2 = 5.40\unit{rad/s}$, so each mass rings about $2.9$ times in the time it takes to hand the energy over. Weaker coupling makes $\Delta\omega$ smaller and the handover slower.

Any motion is a sum of the two modes with four constants fixed by the four initial conditions, and starting one mass alone makes the energy slosh back and forth with period $2\pi/\Delta\omega$.

Untangling the equations by hand

There is a second route to everything above, and it is worth seeing because it is diagonalisation done in plain sight, without a matrix anywhere.

Two independent oscillator equations, of exactly the form solved in section 2.1, with the two frequencies of (2.2.5) sitting in them. The variables $q_1$ and $q_2$ are the normal coordinates. Solve each one separately, then recover the masses from $x_1 = q_1 + q_2$ and $x_2 = q_1 - q_2$.

And this is not a different method. It is the change of basis of section 1.3, written out.

Worked example

Check the last line with numbers. $\mat K = \begin{pmatrix} 15 & -5 \\ -5 & 15\end{pmatrix}$, $\mat P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1\end{pmatrix}$.

$\mat K \mat P$ has columns $\mat K(1,1) = (10, 10)$ and $\mat K(1,-1) = (20, -20)$, so $\mat K\mat P = \begin{pmatrix} 10 & 20 \\ 10 & -20\end{pmatrix}$.

Then $\mat P^{-1}(\mat K\mat P)$: first column $= \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac12 & \tfrac12 \\ \tfrac12 & -\tfrac12\end{pmatrix}(10,10) = (10, 0)$; second column $= \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac12 & \tfrac12 \\ \tfrac12 & -\tfrac12\end{pmatrix}(20,-20) = (0, 20)$.

So $\mat P^{-1}\mat K\mat P = \begin{pmatrix} 10 & 0 \\ 0 & 20\end{pmatrix}\unit{N/m}$ — that is $k$ and $k + 2\kappa$, exactly as the derivation said. Dividing by $m = 0.5\unit{kg}$ gives $\omega_1^{2} = 20$ and $\omega_2^{2} = 40$. ✓

Adding and subtracting the two equations gives two uncoupled oscillators; that is diagonalisation, carried out by hand.

When the two sides are not the same

Equal masses and equal wall springs made the arithmetic clean and put the modes at $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$. Nothing about the method needed that. With masses $m_1, m_2$ and wall springs $k_1, k_2$ the matrices become

$$ \mat M = \begin{pmatrix} m_1 & 0 \\ 0 & m_2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mat K = \begin{pmatrix} k_1 + \kappa & -\kappa \\ -\kappa & k_2 + \kappa \end{pmatrix} $$
(2.2.6)

Solve the quadratic for $\omega^{2}$, take square roots, put each back to find its mode shape. The shapes will no longer be $(1,\pm 1)$ — the heavier mass moves less — but they will still be two, still real, and still independent, because $\mat K$ is still symmetric for the same third-law reason.

Unequal masses and springs change the numbers and the mode shapes, but not the method: it is always a determinant set to zero, giving a quadratic in $\omega^{2}$.

Check yourself
  1. What happens to $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ as $\kappa \to 0$, and why must it?
    Answer

    Both approach $\sqrt{k/m}$. With no middle spring the masses are two independent oscillators on identical wall springs, so of course they share one frequency; the splitting between the modes is caused entirely by the coupling.

  2. Now let $k \to 0$ instead, leaving only the middle spring. What is $\omega_1$, and what motion does it describe?
    Answer

    $\omega_1 = \sqrt{k/m} \to 0$. The in-phase mode is both masses sliding together with the middle spring unstretched — and with no wall springs there is nothing to pull them back, so they simply drift at constant velocity. A zero-frequency mode is a free translation, not an oscillation. The other mode survives at $\omega_2 = \sqrt{2\kappa/m}$.

  3. Both masses are held at $x_1 = x_2 = 0.03\unit{m}$ and released from rest. Describe the motion, and give its frequency for $m = 0.5\unit{kg}$, $k = 10\unit{N/m}$, $\kappa = 5\unit{N/m}$.
    Answer

    The starting displacement is exactly $0.03\,(1,1)$, a pure first mode, so the system stays in that mode for ever: both masses oscillate together with amplitude $0.03\unit{m}$ at $\omega_1 = 4.472\unit{rad/s}$. The middle spring never stretches, and no energy is ever handed over.

    Formally, $C_3 = (x_1(0)-x_2(0))/2 = 0$ and $C_4 = 0$, so the second mode has zero amplitude.

  4. Show that the two mode shapes are perpendicular, and say which property of the system guaranteed it in advance.
    Answer

    $(1,1)\cdot(1,-1) = 1 - 1 = 0$.

    It was guaranteed by $\mat K$ being symmetric — which in turn came from Newton's third law, since one spring joins the masses and must act equally on both ends. The proof is in section 1.3: eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix with different eigenvalues are always perpendicular.

  5. Mass 1 is pulled aside and released. How long until it is momentarily at rest with all the energy in mass 2? Use $m = 0.5\unit{kg}$, $k = 10\unit{N/m}$, $\kappa = 5\unit{N/m}$.
    Answer

    $\Delta\omega = 6.325 - 4.472 = 1.853\unit{rad/s}$. The envelope $\cos(\Delta\omega\,t/2)$ first vanishes at $t = \pi/\Delta\omega = 1.70\unit{s}$. The full round trip takes $2\pi/\Delta\omega = 3.39\unit{s}$.
