Second-order linear differential equations

Newton's second law is a statement about acceleration, so almost every equation in mechanics is second order. This page is about what that costs you — two free constants — and how to find the solutions without guessing blindly.

Linear means solutions add

An equation is linear if the unknown function and its derivatives appear only to the first power, never multiplied together, never inside a sine or a square root. So $\ddot x + \omega^{2}x = 0$ is linear; $\ddot x + x^{2} = 0$ is not, and neither is $\ddot x + \sin x = 0$ — which is the exact pendulum, and is exactly why the small-angle approximation gets made.

Linearity buys one enormous thing: solutions can be added.

Now watch the same attempt fail for a non-linear equation, because seeing where it breaks is more useful than being told that it does.

Every superposition argument on this site — adding normal modes, adding harmonics into a Fourier series, adding the waves from two slits — rests on the first derivation and would collapse under the second.

In a linear equation any sum of solutions is another solution; the cross terms of a non-linear one destroy that immediately.

Why second order means two constants

The order of a differential equation is the highest derivative in it. The rule is that the order equals the number of free constants in the general solution, and the reason is simply that undoing a derivative always leaves one.

A second-order equation needs two initial numbers, because undoing two derivatives leaves two constants.

Solving it: guess an exponential

There is no general recipe for solving differential equations, but for linear ones with constant coefficients there is, and it is a guess that always works. The reason it works: differentiating $e^{rt}$ gives back $e^{rt}$ times a number, so every term in the equation ends up with the same exponential factor and it cancels.

That quadratic is called the characteristic equation. Two roots means two solutions, $e^{+i\omega t}$ and $e^{-i\omega t}$, and by superposition any combination of them is a solution too. Section 1.1 turns those into real functions.

The same method handles damping, which is worth seeing once even though the rest of this site does not need it.

Guessing $e^{rt}$ turns a linear differential equation with constant coefficients into a polynomial, whose roots give the solutions.

The same solution, written three ways

You will meet the general solution of $\ddot x + \omega^{2}x = 0$ in three costumes. They are the same function, and being able to move between them quickly is worth the ten minutes it takes to see why.

$$ x(t) = C_1\cos\omega t + C_2 \sin\omega t \;=\; A\cos(\omega t + \varphi) \;=\; \operatorname{Re}\!\left[c\,e^{i\omega t}\right] $$
(1.2.1)

So the complex amplitude $c$ carries both constants at once: its length is $A$ and minus its angle is $\varphi$. One complex number, two real facts.

Amplitude-and-phase, cosine-plus-sine, and a complex amplitude are three names for the same two constants.

Fixing the constants from the start

This also settles a question that trips people up: why is $x = A\cos\omega t$ not the general solution? Because it has one constant, and setting $t=0$ in its derivative gives $\dot x(0) = 0$ always. It can only describe motions released from rest. Any problem where the object is given a push at $t = 0$ needs the sine term as well.

Worked example

$\ddot x + 9x = 0$ with $x(0) = 0.2\unit{m}$ and $\dot x(0) = -0.9\unit{m/s}$. Here $\omega = 3\unit{rad/s}$.

$C_1 = 0.2\unit{m}$ and $C_2 = -0.9/3 = -0.3\unit{m}$, so $x(t) = 0.2\cos 3t - 0.3\sin 3t$ metres.

In amplitude-and-phase form: $A = \sqrt{0.04 + 0.09} = 0.3606\unit{m}$ and $\tan\varphi = -(-0.3)/0.2 = 1.5$, so $\varphi = 56.31^{\circ}$ and $x(t) = 0.361\cos(3t + 56.31^{\circ})\unit{m}$.

Check at $t = 0$: $0.361\cos(56.31^{\circ}) = 0.361 \times 0.5547 = 0.200$. ✓

The cosine coefficient is the starting position and the sine coefficient is the starting velocity divided by $\omega$; a one-constant solution can only describe a start from rest.

More than one moving thing

Everything above was one unknown function. When two objects influence each other you get two coupled second-order equations, and the counting still holds: two equations of second order need four constants, which is exactly the two positions and two velocities you would have to specify to start such a system.

The method survives too. Guess that everything oscillates at one shared frequency, with a vector of amplitudes instead of a single one — $\vb x = \vb a\,e^{i\omega t}$ — and the exponential cancels exactly as it did here, leaving algebra. That algebra turns out to be the eigenvalue problem of section 1.3, and section 2.2 carries it through.

The same exponential guess, with a vector amplitude, handles systems of coupled oscillators.

Check yourself
  1. Which of these are linear: $\ddot x + 4x = 0$, $\ddot x + x^{2} = 0$, $\ddot x + t^{2}x = 0$?
    Answer
    The first and the third. Linearity is about how $x$ appears, not $t$: a coefficient that varies with time is still linear, and superposition still holds. Only $x^{2}$ breaks it.
  2. Verify that $e^{-i\omega t}$ solves $\ddot x + \omega^{2}x = 0$.
    Answer
    Each derivative brings down $-i\omega$, so two bring down $(-i\omega)^{2} = i^{2}\omega^{2} = -\omega^{2}$. Then $-\omega^{2}e^{-i\omega t} + \omega^{2}e^{-i\omega t} = 0$. ✓
  3. Solve $\ddot x + 16x = 0$ with $x(0) = 1$, $\dot x(0) = 0$.
    Answer
    $\omega = 4$, so $C_1 = 1$ and $C_2 = 0/4 = 0$: $x(t) = \cos 4t$. Released from rest, so no sine term.
  4. Why can no choice of $A$ make $A\cos\omega t$ satisfy $x(0) = 0$ with $\dot x(0) = 1$?
    Answer
    $x(0) = A$ forces $A = 0$, and then $x$ is zero for all time, so $\dot x(0) = 0 \ne 1$. One constant cannot meet two conditions. The correct solution is $x = (1/\omega)\sin\omega t$.
  5. Give the general solution of $\ddot x - 4x = 0$. Why is it not an oscillation?
    Answer
    The characteristic equation is $r^{2} - 4 = 0$, so $r = \pm 2$ — real, not imaginary — and $x = C_1e^{2t} + C_2e^{-2t}$. A real exponent means growth and decay rather than oscillation. Physically the sign has flipped: the force pushes the object away from the origin instead of back towards it.
