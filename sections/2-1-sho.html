The simple harmonic oscillator

One mass, one spring. Everything on the rest of this site is built out of this, so it is worth doing slowly: where the equation comes from, why its solution needs two constants, and what the energy is doing while the mass moves.

You will need

Section 1.1 for complex exponentials and section 1.2 for the exponential guess. On this page $k$ is a spring constant; from section 2.3 onwards it will be a wavenumber instead.

Where the equation comes from

A mass $m$ on a spring of stiffness $k$, on a frictionless surface, with $x$ measured from the position where the spring is unstretched. Hooke's law says the spring pulls back in proportion to how far it has been stretched: $F = -kx$, the minus sign meaning the force always points back towards $x = 0$.

Note what the frequency does not depend on: how far you pulled the mass. A stiff spring or a light mass gives a fast oscillation, and that is the whole story. The amplitude is set by how you started it and never affects the timing.

Worked example

$k = 200\unit{N/m}$, $m = 0.5\unit{kg}$.

$\omega = \sqrt{200/0.5} = \sqrt{400} = 20\unit{rad/s}$, so $f = \omega/2\pi = 3.18\unit{Hz}$ and $T = 1/f = 0.314\unit{s}$.

Quadruple the mass and $\omega$ halves, so the period doubles.

Hooke's law plus Newton's second law gives $\ddot x + \omega^{2}x = 0$ with $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$, and the frequency does not depend on how hard you started it.

Checking the solution, and the three forms

Section 1.2 solved this equation with the exponential guess and got $x = C_1\cos\omega t + C_2\sin\omega t$. It is worth substituting the amplitude-and-phase form back in directly, because the mechanism — each derivative pulling down a factor of $\omega$ — is the same one that will do the work in every later section.

The three ways of writing it, from section 1.2, all appear in the literature and all mean the same motion:

$$ x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \varphi) \;=\; C_1\cos\omega t + C_2\sin\omega t \;=\; \operatorname{Re}\!\left[c\,e^{i\omega t}\right] $$
(2.1.1)

Two derivatives of a sinusoid give back the same sinusoid times $-\omega^{2}$, which is exactly what the equation of motion asks for.

Starting it off

Section 1.2 showed $C_1 = x(0)$ and $C_2 = \dot x(0)/\omega$. Converting those to amplitude and phase is worth having explicitly, because it is the form you actually want when you are told a mass was released with a given speed.

Read the amplitude formula physically. Even a mass released at $x_0 = 0$ has an amplitude, $v_0/\omega$, because the speed you gave it carries it out to that distance before the spring stops it. Conversely a mass released from rest at $x_0$ has amplitude exactly $x_0$. The two contributions add in quadrature — as squares — because the position and the velocity are a quarter cycle out of step, which section 1.1 drew as a right angle.

Worked example

$k = 200\unit{N/m}$, $m = 0.5\unit{kg}$, so $\omega = 20\unit{rad/s}$. The mass is at $x_0 = 0.05\unit{m}$ and moving at $v_0 = -1.0\unit{m/s}$.

$C_1 = 0.05\unit{m}$, $C_2 = -1.0/20 = -0.05\unit{m}$.

$A = \sqrt{0.05^{2} + 0.05^{2}} = 0.0707\unit{m}$ and $\tan\varphi = -(-0.05)/0.05 = 1$, so $\varphi = 45^{\circ}$ and $x(t) = 0.0707\cos(20t + 45^{\circ})\unit{m}$.

Check: $x(0) = 0.0707\cos 45^{\circ} = 0.0500\unit{m}$ ✓, and $\dot x(0) = -0.0707 \times 20 \times \sin 45^{\circ} = -1.00\unit{m/s}$ ✓.

The amplitude is $\sqrt{x_0^{2} + (v_0/\omega)^{2}}$: position and velocity both contribute, and they add as squares because they are a quarter cycle apart.

Energy

The kinetic energy is $\half m\dot x^{2}$ and the energy stored in a spring stretched by $x$ is $\half k x^{2}$ — the work $\int_0^x kx'\,\dd x'$ done against it. Their sum is constant, and there are two ways to see that.

So the energy depends on the amplitude alone, and it moves back and forth between the two forms twice per cycle: all potential at the turning points where the mass is momentarily still, all kinetic as it flies through the middle.

Worked example

With $A = 0.0707\unit{m}$ and $k = 200\unit{N/m}$: $E = \half(200)(0.0707)^{2} = 0.500\unit{J}$.

Check it against the starting conditions directly: $\half(0.5)(1.0)^{2} + \half(200)(0.05)^{2} = 0.250 + 0.250 = 0.500\unit{J}$ ✓ — and note the energy happened to be exactly half kinetic and half potential at that instant, which is why the phase came out at $45^{\circ}$.

Total energy is $\half kA^{2}$, fixed by the amplitude alone, and it shuttles between kinetic and potential twice each cycle.

Check yourself
  1. The mass is doubled and the spring left alone. What happens to the period?
    Answer
    $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$ falls by $\sqrt2$, so the period $T = 2\pi/\omega$ grows by $\sqrt2 = 1.414$. It does not double.
  2. A mass is at $x = 0$ at $t = 0$ and moving at $v_0$. Give $x(t)$ and its amplitude.
    Answer
    $C_1 = 0$ and $C_2 = v_0/\omega$, so $x(t) = (v_0/\omega)\sin\omega t$ and $A = v_0/\omega$. The faster the oscillator, the less far a given kick carries it.
  3. Write $2\cos 5t - 2\sin 5t$ as a single cosine.
    Answer
    $A = \sqrt{4+4} = 2\sqrt2 = 2.828$ and $\tan\varphi = -(-2)/2 = 1$, so $\varphi = 45^{\circ}$ and it is $2\sqrt2\cos(5t + 45^{\circ})$.
  4. For $x = A\cos\omega t$, show the energy at $t = 0$ equals the energy at $t = T/4$.
    Answer
    At $t=0$: the mass is at $x = A$ and still, so $E = \half kA^{2}$, all potential. At $t = T/4$ the phase is $\pi/2$: $x = 0$ and $\dot x = -A\omega$, so $E = \half m A^{2}\omega^{2} = \half kA^{2}$, all kinetic. The same, as the derivation promised.
  5. Which of $\omega$, $A$, $\varphi$, $E$ is fixed by the apparatus rather than by how you start it?
    Answer
    Only $\omega$, which depends on $k$ and $m$ alone. The amplitude and phase come from the initial conditions, and the energy $\half kA^{2}$ depends on the amplitude, so it does too.
