Fourier series and Fourier transforms

Any repeating shape, however jagged, is a sum of sines and cosines. Section 1.3 already explained why that is possible and how to find the amounts; this page carries it through, and then takes the limit where the repetition goes away.

Note on letters

From here on $k$ means a wavenumber, $2\pi$ divided by a wavelength — not a spring constant. $T$ is a period and $\omega_0 = 2\pi/T$ the fundamental angular frequency.

Finding the amounts

Suppose a function repeats with period $T$. The claim is that it can be written as a constant plus a sum of cosines and sines whose frequencies are whole multiples of $\omega_0 = 2\pi/T$:

$$ f(t) = a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left[a_n\cos n\omega_0 t + b_n \sin n\omega_0 t\right] $$
(1.4.1)

Only whole multiples appear, because anything else would not repeat with period $T$: a frequency $1.5\,\omega_0$ would take two periods to come back to where it started, so the sum would not have the period we assumed.

Section 1.3 established the fact that makes the coefficients findable: over one period, any two different harmonics are perpendicular, and every sine is perpendicular to every cosine. So take the inner product with the one you want and everything else drops out.

So $a_0$ is simply the average value of $f$ over a period — its DC offset, in the language of circuits.

Worked example

$f(t) = 2 + 3\cos\omega_0 t - \sin 2\omega_0 t$ is already a Fourier series, so its coefficients can be read off: $a_0 = 2$, $a_1 = 3$, $b_2 = -1$, everything else zero.

Confirm $a_1$ from the integral: $\frac{2}{T}\int_0^T f\cos\omega_0 t\,\dd t$. The constant contributes nothing, the $-\sin 2\omega_0 t$ contributes nothing, and the $3\cos\omega_0 t$ contributes $\frac{2}{T} \times 3 \times \frac{T}{2} = 3$. ✓

Each coefficient is found by multiplying by the harmonic you want and integrating: every other term is perpendicular to it and disappears.

What symmetry tells you before you integrate

Often half the coefficients are zero for reasons you can see by looking at the shape, and knowing which half saves the work of finding out.

The same argument with the roles swapped shows an odd function has no cosine terms and no constant. Cosines are even and sines are odd, so a function built only of cosines is even and one built only of sines is odd — the series cannot help but respect the symmetry.

An even function has only cosines, an odd one only sines, and a shape that inverts after half a period has only odd harmonics.

The square wave, and what changes when you narrow it

Take the square wave that sits at $+1$ for the first half of each period and $-1$ for the second. It is odd, so only sines appear, and it inverts after half a period, so only odd harmonics appear. That leaves one integral to do.

So the square wave is $\frac{4}{\pi}\left[\sin\omega_0 t + \frac13\sin 3\omega_0 t + \frac15 \sin 5\omega_0 t + \cdots\right]$: only odd harmonics, falling off as $1/n$. The slow fall-off is the price of the vertical jumps — a sharp corner needs high frequencies.

A narrower pulse changes everything

Now take a pulse that is on for only a quarter of each period, and sits at $0$ rather than $-1$ the rest of the time. It is neither odd nor half-wave symmetric any more, so both of the shortcuts above are lost — and the coefficients say so.

Check the two cases against each other. At $d = \tfrac12$ the formula gives $a_n = \frac{2}{n\pi}\sin(n\pi/2)$, which is zero for even $n$ and $\pm 2/(n\pi)$ for odd $n$ — the odd-harmonic-only pattern again, at half the size because this pulse swings between 0 and 1 rather than $-1$ and $+1$.

At $d = \tfrac14$, though, $a_2 = \frac{2}{2\pi}\sin(\pi/2) = 1/\pi = 0.318$, which is not zero: the even harmonics have come back, because the shape no longer inverts after half a period. And $a_0 = 0.25$ rather than $0$: a DC offset has appeared, because the pulse spends more time at 0 than at 1.

Worked example

Half duty, $\pm1$ square wave: $b_1 = 4/\pi = 1.273$, $b_3 = 4/3\pi = 0.424$, $b_5 = 4/5\pi = 0.255$, and $b_2 = b_4 = 0$.

Quarter duty, 0-to-1 pulse: $a_0 = 0.250$, $a_1 = \frac{2}{\pi}\sin 45^{\circ} = 0.450$, $a_2 = \frac{1}{\pi}\sin 90^{\circ} = 0.318$, $a_3 = \frac{2}{3\pi}\sin 135^{\circ} = 0.150$, and $a_4 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sin 180^{\circ} = 0$.

The fourth harmonic vanishes for a different reason than before: $n\pi d$ hits a multiple of $\pi$ exactly when $n$ is a multiple of $1/d = 4$.

The square wave needs only odd harmonics falling as $1/n$; narrowing the pulse breaks that symmetry, so the even harmonics and a constant term return.

When the repetition goes away

A Fourier series needs a repeating function. A single pulse — one wave packet, one flash of light, one slit — does not repeat. The fix is to let the period grow without bound, and watch what the series becomes.

First rewrite the series in exponential form, which halves the bookkeeping.

Now let $T$ grow. Since $\omega_0 = 2\pi/T$, the allowed frequencies $n\omega_0$ crowd closer and closer together, and in the limit every frequency is allowed.

The pair of formulas is the Fourier transform and its inverse. Written in space rather than time, which is the form the wave pages need:

$$ F(k) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,e^{-ikx}\;\dd x, \qquad f(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} F(k)\,e^{ikx}\;\dd k $$
(1.4.2)

$F(k)$ says how much of the wave $e^{ikx}$ is present in $f$. It is the same statement as a Fourier coefficient — a component along a basis direction — with the basis now continuous.

The zeros of that transform are at $ka/2 = \pi, 2\pi, \ldots$, that is at $k = 2\pi/a, 4\pi/a, \ldots$. Narrow the rectangle and its transform spreads out. That reciprocity is the subject of the next subsection and, in different clothes, of sections 2.6 and 2.8.

Letting the period grow without bound turns the sum over harmonics into an integral over all frequencies: that integral is the Fourier transform.

The Gaussian, and the width trade-off

One shape transforms into itself: the Gaussian $e^{-x^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}$. It is worth doing because it gives the cleanest possible statement of the reciprocity between a pulse's width and its spectrum's width — and because that statement, dressed in physical constants, is the uncertainty principle.

First an integral that is needed on the way, and that cannot be done by finding an antiderivative because it does not have one in elementary terms.

A Gaussian of width $\sigma$ in $x$ has become a Gaussian of width $1/\sigma$ in $k$. The narrower the pulse, the broader its spectrum, and the product of the two widths is fixed. Making that precise needs a definition of "width": the standard deviation of $\abs{f}^{2}$, which is what a physical measurement of a spread responds to.

Worked example

A pulse with $\sigma = 2\unit{mm}$ has $\Delta x = 2/\sqrt2 = 1.414\unit{mm}$ and $\Delta k = 1/(2\sqrt2) = 0.354\unit{mm^{-1}}$. Their product is $0.500$. ✓

Squeeze it to $\sigma = 0.2\unit{mm}$: now $\Delta x = 0.141\unit{mm}$ and $\Delta k = 3.54\unit{mm^{-1}}$ — ten times narrower in space, ten times wider in wavenumber, and the same product.

A Gaussian transforms into a Gaussian, and the product of the widths in $x$ and in $k$ is exactly one half — no shape does better.

Check yourself
  1. A sawtooth rises linearly from $-1$ to $+1$ over each period and drops instantly back. Which coefficients vanish, and why?
    Answer
    It is odd about the centre of its rise, so all $a_n$ vanish and $a_0 = 0$; only sine terms survive. It does not have half-wave symmetry, so both even and odd harmonics appear.
  2. Which harmonics does $\abs{\sin\omega_0 t}$ contain?
    Answer
    Taking the absolute value halves the period, so what was the second harmonic is now the fundamental: only even multiples of $\omega_0$ appear. It is also even in time, so only cosines, and its average is not zero — $a_0 = 2/\pi$.
  3. What is $b_1$ for a square wave that swings between $-5$ and $+5$?
    Answer
    The coefficients scale with the function, so $b_1 = 5 \times 4/\pi = 20/\pi = 6.37$.
  4. A pulse is made twice as narrow. What happens to its spectrum?
    Answer
    It becomes twice as wide, and the product of the widths is unchanged. For a rectangle the first zero moves from $2\pi/a$ to $4\pi/a$; for a Gaussian $\Delta k$ doubles.
  5. Where is the first zero of the transform of a rectangle of width $a$?
    Answer
    $F(k) = a\operatorname{sinc}(ka/2)$ vanishes when $ka/2 = \pi$, so $k = 2\pi/a$. Section 2.8 meets the same expression as the first dark fringe of a single slit.
