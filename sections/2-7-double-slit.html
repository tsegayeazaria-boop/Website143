Double-slit interference

Two slits, one wave, and a screen. Everything on this page comes from asking how far the wave had to travel to reach a given point by each route, and adding the two arrivals with the phasor arithmetic of section 1.1.

The extra distance

Light of one wavelength falls on a barrier with two narrow slits a distance $d$ apart. A screen sits a distance $L$ away. Each slit acts as a source of waves — section 2.8 explains why — and a point on the screen receives one wave from each. The two have travelled different distances, so they arrive out of step by an amount that depends on where you look.

The assumption $L \gg d$ is called the far-field condition. It is what lets two rays that actually converge on one point be treated as parallel, and it holds comfortably in a laboratory: slits a quarter of a millimetre apart, a screen two metres away.

In the far field the two paths differ by $d\sin\theta$, and that single length controls everything else.

Where it is bright and where it is dark

Now convert angles into positions on the screen, which is what you actually measure.

$$ \Delta y = \frac{\lambda L}{d} $$
(2.7.1)

Read the three dependences. Longer wavelength, wider fringes — so red fringes are more spread out than blue. Further screen, wider fringes, simply because the whole pattern is being projected larger. Wider slit separation, narrower fringes: pushing the slits apart squeezes the pattern together, which is the opposite of what most people guess.

Worked example

A helium–neon laser, $\lambda = 633\unit{nm}$, slits $d = 0.25\unit{mm}$ apart, screen $L = 2.00\unit{m}$ away.

$\Delta y = (633\times10^{-9})(2.00)/(0.25\times10^{-3}) = 5.06\times10^{-3}\unit{m} = 5.06\unit{mm}$ — comfortably visible.

The first-order fringe is at $\theta$ with $\sin\theta = \lambda/d = 2.53\times10^{-3}$, that is $0.145^{\circ}$. The small-angle approximation is excellent here: $\sin$ and $\tan$ of that angle agree to better than one part in a million.

Bright fringes sit where $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$, evenly spaced on the screen by $\Delta y = \lambda L/d$.

How bright it is in between

Knowing where the fringes are is not the whole pattern. To get the brightness everywhere, add the two waves properly — which is exactly the phasor sum derived in section 1.1.

Brightness is not amplitude. What a detector or an eye responds to is the average over many cycles of the square of the field, and that average is worth doing once.

$$ I(\theta) = I_0 \cos^{2}\!\left(\frac{\pi d\sin\theta}{\lambda}\right) $$
(2.7.2)

One thing worth noticing: the peak brightness is $4E_0^{2}$, four times what one slit alone would give — not twice. And the dark fringes are exactly zero. Averaged across the screen it comes to $2E_0^{2}$, which is what two slits' worth of energy should give: the interference moves the light around without creating or destroying any.

Worked example

With the numbers above, the fringe spacing was $5.06\unit{mm}$. What is the brightness a quarter of the way from a bright fringe to the next dark one — at $y = 1.27\unit{mm}$?

$\sin\theta \approx y/L = 6.33\times10^{-4}$, so $\delta = 2\pi(0.25\times10^{-3})(6.33\times10^{-4})/(633\times10^{-9}) = \pi/2$.

$I = I_0\cos^{2}(\pi/4) = I_0/2$. Half brightness, a quarter of the way from a maximum to a minimum.

The brightness is $I_0\cos^{2}(\delta/2)$ with $\delta = 2\pi d\sin\theta/\lambda$: full cancellation in the dark fringes and four times one slit's brightness in the bright ones.

Check yourself
  1. Find the fringe spacing for $\lambda = 500\unit{nm}$, $L = 1.5\unit{m}$, $d = 0.30\unit{mm}$.
    Answer
    $\Delta y = \lambda L/d = (500\times10^{-9})(1.5)/(3\times10^{-4}) = 2.5\times10^{-3}\unit{m} = 2.5\unit{mm}$.
  2. Red light or blue light — which gives wider fringes, and why?
    Answer
    Red, since $\Delta y \propto \lambda$ and red light has the longer wavelength. It needs a larger angle to accumulate one wavelength of extra path.
  3. The whole apparatus is submerged in water, refractive index $n = 1.33$. What happens to the pattern?
    Answer
    The frequency is set by the source and does not change, but the speed drops by a factor $n$, so the wavelength does too: $\lambda' = \lambda/n$. The fringes therefore crowd together by the same factor, $\Delta y' = \Delta y/1.33$.
  4. At what phase difference is the brightness a quarter of its peak?
    Answer
    $\cos^{2}(\delta/2) = \tfrac14$ needs $\cos(\delta/2) = \tfrac12$, so $\delta/2 = \pi/3$ and $\delta = 2\pi/3$ — a third of a cycle out of step.
  5. If one slit were made narrower than the other, would the dark fringes still be completely dark?
    Answer
    No. The cancellation in the derivation relied on the two amplitudes being equal, so that $E_0 - E_0 = 0$. With unequal amplitudes the total at a minimum is $E_1 - E_2 \neq 0$, and the fringes have a dim floor rather than reaching zero. The bright fringes also become less bright relative to the background.
