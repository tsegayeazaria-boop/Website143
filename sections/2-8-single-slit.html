Single-slit diffraction

Section 2.7 assumed each slit was a point source. It is not, and this page is about what its actual width does. The answer is the most counter-intuitive result on the site: narrowing a slit makes the light coming out of it spread wider.

Every point in the slit is a source

Huygens' idea: every point of a wavefront acts as a source of a new wave, and the wave further on is the sum of all of them. Applied to a slit of width $a$, that means a continuous line of sources filling the slit, all in step with each other, all sending waves towards the screen.

Finding the dark places is easier than it looks, because they can be found by pairing sources off.

Note carefully what $m = 0$ is not. In section 2.7, $m = 0$ was the central bright fringe. Here the formula gives minima, and $m = 0$ is excluded — straight ahead every source is in step with every other, which is the brightest point of all. The pairing argument breaks down there because the path difference is zero for every pair.

Worked example

$a = 0.10\unit{mm}$, $\lambda = 633\unit{nm}$, screen at $L = 2.0\unit{m}$.

First minimum: $\sin\theta = \lambda/a = 6.33\times10^{-3}$, so $\theta = 0.363^{\circ}$.

On the screen that is $y_1 = L\tan\theta = 12.7\unit{mm}$, so the central bright band is $25.3\unit{mm}$ wide — from the first dark fringe on one side to the first on the other.

Pairing each source with one half a slit away shows the whole slit cancels when $a\sin\theta = m\lambda$, for every non-zero integer $m$.

Narrower slit, wider pattern

The slit width is in the denominator. Halve $a$ and the patch of light doubles in width. That is the opposite of the geometric intuition — that a narrower hole should give a narrower beam — and it is worth being clear about why the intuition fails: light is not made of rays travelling in straight lines. A narrower slit forces the wave into a smaller region, and section 1.4 already showed what that costs.

This retrospectively justifies section 2.7. There the two slits were treated as point sources whose light spread across the whole screen — which is exactly what happens when each slit is narrow enough that its own first minimum is off the screen entirely.

The central band is about $2\lambda/a$ wide in angle, so narrowing the slit widens the pattern; at $a = \lambda$ it fills the whole half-space.

The brightness everywhere

The pairing argument gives the minima but says nothing about how bright it is between them. For that, add up all the sources properly. There are infinitely many, so the sum is an integral — and section 1.4 has already done this integral.

$$ I(\theta) = I_0\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^{2}, \qquad \beta = \frac{\pi a \sin\theta}{\lambda} $$
(2.8.1)
Worked example

How bright is the first side band, halfway between the first and second minima?

Those sit at $\beta = \pi$ and $\beta = 2\pi$, so try $\beta = 3\pi/2$: $\sin\beta = -1$, and $I/I_0 = (1/(3\pi/2))^{2} = (0.2122)^{2} = 0.045$.

Under five per cent of the central peak. The true maximum is very slightly closer in, at $\beta = 4.493$, where $I/I_0 = 0.0472$. Almost all of the light is in the central band.

And at $\beta = \pi/2$, halfway out to the first minimum: $I/I_0 = (1/(\pi/2))^{2} = 0.405$.

Summing every point of the slit gives $I = I_0(\sin\beta/\beta)^{2}$, whose zeros are exactly the minima the pairing argument found.

Two other ways to see it

The result of the last subsection was not a new calculation. It was the transform of a rectangle from section 1.4, and that is worth saying explicitly.

That is a general statement, not one about slits: the far-field pattern of any aperture is the Fourier transform of its shape. A circular hole gives the transform of a disc, which is why stars seen through a telescope are little rings.

And read through section 1.4's width relation, the same fact is the uncertainty principle.

So the counter-intuitive result of this page — narrower slit, wider spread — is the uncertainty principle seen in ordinary light. Confining the wave sideways forces a spread in its sideways momentum, and that spread is the diffraction. Nothing quantum is needed for the effect; the quantum content is only the substitution $p = \hbar k$.

The far-field pattern of an aperture is the Fourier transform of its shape, and the widening as the slit narrows is the width relation of section 1.4 in physical form.

Check yourself
  1. Find the first minimum for $a = 0.05\unit{mm}$ and $\lambda = 500\unit{nm}$.
    Answer
    $\sin\theta = \lambda/a = 0.01$, so $\theta = 0.573^{\circ}$. On a screen $2\unit{m}$ away that is $y = 20\unit{mm}$, and the central band is $40\unit{mm}$ wide.
  2. The slit is made twice as wide. What happens to the central bright band?
    Answer
    Its width $2\lambda L/a$ halves. More light gets through, and it is concentrated into half the area — so the central band is both narrower and brighter.
  3. Why is there no minimum at $m = 0$?
    Answer
    Straight ahead every part of the slit is exactly in step with every other, so everything adds rather than cancelling. The pairing argument needs a non-zero path difference to work, and $\sin\theta = 0$ gives none.
  4. Is the second minimum at twice the angle of the first?
    Answer
    Its sine is twice as large: $\sin\theta_2 = 2\lambda/a$. For small angles that makes the angle very nearly double, but for wide angles it does not — and if $2\lambda/a$ exceeds one there is no second minimum at all.
  5. How does $a\sin\theta = \lambda$ relate to $\Delta x\,\Delta p \gtrsim \hbar$?
    Answer
    They are the same statement. The slit fixes the sideways position to $\Delta x \approx a$; the spread of directions out to the first minimum is $\Delta k_\perp \approx k\lambda/a = 2\pi/a$; their product is about $2\pi$, independent of $a$. Multiplying by $\hbar$ turns it into the momentum form.
