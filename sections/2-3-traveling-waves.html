Travelling waves and plane waves

A wave is an oscillation that also moves. Everything on this page follows from writing that down carefully and asking what each symbol has to mean.

Letters change here

From this page onwards $k$ is a wavenumber, measured in radians per metre — not the spring constant of sections 2.1 and 2.2. String tension is written $\Ten$ so that $T$ can go on meaning a period.

A wave repeats in two ways at once

Freeze time and photograph a wave on a string: you see a shape that repeats along the string, with spatial period $\lambda$, the wavelength. Now stand at one point and watch: the string there goes up and down, repeating with period $T$. The same function has to do both, which fixes what can appear inside the cosine.

$$ y(x,t) = A\cos(kx - \omega t + \varphi) $$
(2.3.1)

$k = 2\pi/\lambda$ counts phase per metre and $\omega = 2\pi/T$ counts phase per second.

Which way it goes, and how fast

To follow a particular crest, follow a point of constant phase — the argument of the cosine held fixed while both $x$ and $t$ change.

Worked example

A wave on a string has $\lambda = 0.5\unit{m}$ and $f = 4\unit{Hz}$.

$k = 2\pi/0.5 = 12.57\unit{rad/m}$, $\omega = 2\pi(4) = 25.13\unit{rad/s}$, and $v = \omega/k = 2.00\unit{m/s}$ — which is also $\lambda f = 0.5 \times 4$. ✓

Written out: $y(x,t) = A\cos(12.57x - 25.13t)$ with $x$ in metres and $t$ in seconds.

A crest moves at $v = \omega/k = \lambda f$, and the sign inside the cosine decides which way.

What is actually oscillating

Equation (2.3.1) says how much of something there is at position $x$ and time $t$. What that something is depends on the wave, and the direction it points relative to the direction of travel divides waves into two families.

In a transverse wave the oscillation is perpendicular to the direction of travel: a string flicked sideways, or light, where the electric field points across the beam. In a longitudinal wave it is along the direction of travel: sound, where the air is alternately squeezed and stretched in the direction the sound goes.

The mathematics does not care. Both are described by (2.3.1); only the physical meaning of $y$ changes, and with it what "amplitude" is measured in — metres of sideways displacement for the string, pascals of pressure for the sound.

Transverse and longitudinal waves obey the same equation; only the direction of the oscillating quantity differs.

Waves in three dimensions

In three dimensions the position is a vector, and $kx$ becomes a dot product: $y = A\cos(\vb k \cdot \vb r - \omega t)$. The vector $\vb k$ carries both pieces of information at once — its direction is the direction of travel, and its length is the wavenumber.

Worked example

$\vb k = (3, 4)\unit{rad/m}$. Its length is $\abs{\vb k} = \sqrt{9+16} = 5\unit{rad/m}$, so $\lambda = 2\pi/5 = 1.257\unit{m}$, and the wave travels at $\arctan(4/3) = 53.1^{\circ}$ above the $x$-axis.

The crests are lines perpendicular to that direction, spaced $1.257\unit{m}$ apart measured along the direction of travel.

In a plane wave the crests are flat sheets perpendicular to $\vb k$, whose length is $2\pi/\lambda$ and whose direction is the direction of travel.

What the medium decides

So far $k$ and $\omega$ have been independent. In a real medium they are not: the physics of the material links them, and that link is the dispersion relation $\omega(k)$.

To see where it comes from, take the wave equation, which section 2.4 derives from Newton's second law for a string:

$$ \frac{\partial^{2} y}{\partial t^{2}} = v^{2}\,\frac{\partial^{2} y}{\partial x^{2}} $$
(2.3.2)

The curly $\partial$ means a partial derivative: differentiate with respect to one variable while holding the other fixed. $\partial y/\partial t$ is how fast the string moves at a fixed place; $\partial y/\partial x$ is the slope of the string at a fixed moment. Nothing more than that is needed here.

Here $\omega$ is proportional to $k$, so $v = \omega/k$ is the same for every wavelength. A medium like that is called non-dispersive: a complicated shape keeps its shape as it travels, because all of its harmonics move at the same speed. A string under tension is like this, and so is light in vacuum.

Other media are not. Deep water waves obey $\omega = \sqrt{g k}$, so long waves outrun short ones. A free quantum particle obeys $\omega = \hbar k^{2}/2m$. In both cases a pulse made of many wavelengths comes apart as it travels, which is the subject of section 2.6.

Worked example

Sound in air, $v = 340\unit{m/s}$, at $f = 1000\unit{Hz}$.

$\omega = 2\pi(1000) = 6283\unit{rad/s}$; from $\omega = vk$, $k = 6283/340 = 18.5\unit{rad/m}$, and $\lambda = 2\pi/k = 0.340\unit{m}$ — which is also $v/f$. ✓

Air is very nearly non-dispersive over the audible range, which is why an orchestra arrives in the right order.

The medium supplies a relation between $\omega$ and $k$; when it is a straight proportion the medium is non-dispersive and every wavelength travels at the same speed.

Check yourself
  1. Which way, and how fast, does $y = 0.02\cos(5x + 3t)$ travel? Give $\lambda$ and $T$.
    Answer
    The plus sign means it travels in the $-x$ direction, at $\omega/k = 3/5 = 0.6\unit{m/s}$. $\lambda = 2\pi/5 = 1.26\unit{m}$ and $T = 2\pi/3 = 2.09\unit{s}$.
  2. A wave has $\lambda = 2\unit{m}$ and $T = 0.5\unit{s}$. What is its speed?
    Answer
    $v = \lambda/T = 4\unit{m/s}$; equivalently $f = 2\unit{Hz}$ and $v = \lambda f$.
  3. Two waves in the same medium have the same $k$ but different $\omega$. Is that possible?
    Answer
    No, unless the medium supports more than one kind of wave. The dispersion relation is a property of the medium, so fixing $k$ fixes $\omega$. (A string can carry both transverse and longitudinal waves, which have different dispersion relations — but they are different waves, not the same one.)
  4. What is a wavefront, and why is a plane wave called plane?
    Answer
    A wavefront is a surface of constant phase — all the points currently at a crest. For $\cos(\vb k\cdot\vb r - \omega t)$ those surfaces are flat planes perpendicular to $\vb k$, because the dot product only constrains the distance measured along $\vb k$.
  5. Where is $y = A\sin(kx - \omega t)$ zero at $t = 0$?
    Answer
    Wherever $\sin kx = 0$, that is $kx = n\pi$, so $x = n\pi/k = n\lambda/2$: the zeros are half a wavelength apart, as they are for any sinusoid.
