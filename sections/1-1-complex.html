Complex numbers and Euler's formula

Physicists solve oscillation problems with complex exponentials and then throw the imaginary part away. That looks like a trick until you see why it works, which is what this page is for.

Numbers with two parts

A complex number $z = x + iy$ carries two real numbers, so it is naturally drawn as a point in a plane: $x$ across, $y$ up. Everything you know about vectors in a plane applies — addition is component by component, and the distance from the origin is $\abs{z} = \sqrt{x^{2} + y^{2}}$, called the modulus.

What makes complex numbers more than two-component vectors is that you can multiply two of them and get a third, using nothing but $i^{2} = -1$ and ordinary algebra.

The complex conjugate $z^{*} = x - iy$ is the reflection of $z$ across the horizontal axis. It is worth one derivation, because the result is used constantly.

Worked example

$z = 3 + 4i$. Then $\abs{z} = \sqrt{9+16} = 5$, and $z^{*} = 3 - 4i$, and $zz^{*} = 9 + 16 = 25 = 5^{2}$. ✓

Its angle from the positive real axis is $\arctan(4/3) = 53.13^{\circ}$.

A complex number is a point in a plane, and multiplying it by its conjugate gives the square of its distance from the origin.

Multiplication turns and stretches

Instead of "across and up", describe a complex number by "how far and at what angle": $z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)$, where $r = \abs{z}$ and $\theta$ is the angle from the positive real axis. Nothing new yet — that is just polar coordinates. But watch what multiplication does in this description.

Worked example

$(1+i)$ has $r = \sqrt2$ and $\theta = 45^{\circ}$. Squaring it should double the angle and square the length: $r^{2} = 2$ at $90^{\circ}$, which is $2i$.

Check by expanding: $(1+i)^{2} = 1 + 2i + i^{2} = 2i$. ✓

And $(1+i)^{8}$: length $(\sqrt2)^{8} = 16$, angle $8 \times 45^{\circ} = 360^{\circ}$, so $16$. Working that out by expanding brackets would take a page.

Multiplying complex numbers multiplies their lengths and adds their angles.

Euler's formula

Adding angles is exactly what exponents do: $e^{A}e^{B} = e^{A+B}$. That is a strong hint that $\cos\theta + i\sin\theta$ is an exponential of something proportional to $\theta$. Here are two derivations of the fact, because it is the single most useful identity on this site.

Two immediate consequences. Setting $\theta \to -\theta$ gives $e^{-i\theta} = \cos\theta - i\sin\theta$, so $e^{-i\theta}$ is the conjugate of $e^{i\theta}$; and therefore $\abs{e^{i\theta}}^{2} = e^{i\theta}e^{-i\theta} = e^{0} = 1$. A complex exponential with a real angle always has length exactly one: it is a point on the unit circle, and multiplying by it is a pure rotation.

Running the formula backwards gives the two identities you will use whenever a cosine needs to become exponentials:

$e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$ is a point on the unit circle at angle $\theta$, so multiplying by it rotates without stretching.

Why oscillation problems are done this way

Two reasons, and both are about making calculus easier.

Differentiating an exponential is multiplication

Taking the real part is safe

Physical displacements are real numbers, so at the end we keep only the real part. That is only legitimate if the real part of a complex solution is itself a solution, which needs proof rather than assertion.

Putting the two together: to solve an oscillation problem, guess $z = c\,e^{i\omega t}$ with $c$ a complex constant, use the fact that each time derivative is a multiplication, solve the resulting algebra for $c$, and take the real part. The complex amplitude $c$ carries the real amplitude as its length and the phase as its angle, so one complex number does the work of two real ones.

Worked example

Write $2\cos\omega t - 2\sin\omega t$ as a single cosine.

Look for a complex $c$ with $\operatorname{Re}\left[c\,e^{i\omega t}\right]$ equal to it. Since $e^{i\omega t} = \cos\omega t + i\sin\omega t$, writing $c = A + iB$ gives $\operatorname{Re}[c e^{i\omega t}] = A\cos\omega t - B\sin\omega t$. Matching: $A = 2$ and $B = 2$, so $c = 2 + 2i$.

In polar form $c$ has length $2\sqrt2 = 2.828$ and angle $45^{\circ}$, so $c = 2\sqrt2\,e^{i\pi/4}$ and $\operatorname{Re}\left[2\sqrt2 e^{i(\omega t + \pi/4)}\right] = 2\sqrt2\cos(\omega t + 45^{\circ})$.

Check at $t=0$: $2\sqrt2\cos 45^{\circ} = 2$, and the original gives $2 - 0 = 2$. ✓

Adding two waves of the same frequency

One more identity, used on every interference page later. Two oscillations of equal amplitude and the same frequency, one lagging the other by a phase $\delta$, add to a single oscillation — and the algebra is a line long in exponential form.

Each time derivative of $e^{i\omega t}$ is a multiplication by $i\omega$, and the real part of a complex solution solves the same real equation — so the complex method costs nothing and turns calculus into algebra.

Check yourself
  1. Give $\abs{3-4i}$ and its angle.
    Answer
    $\abs{z} = 5$; the angle is $\arctan(-4/3) = -53.13^{\circ}$, below the real axis.
  2. Use the angle rule to show $(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^{2} = \cos 2\theta + i\sin 2\theta$.
    Answer
    Both factors have length 1 and angle $\theta$. Lengths multiply to 1, angles add to $2\theta$. In exponential form it is just $\left(e^{i\theta}\right)^{2} = e^{2i\theta}$.
  3. Find $c$ with $\operatorname{Re}\left[c e^{i\omega t}\right] = 3\cos\omega t + 4\sin\omega t$, and give the amplitude and phase.
    Answer
    $\operatorname{Re}[(A+iB)e^{i\omega t}] = A\cos\omega t - B\sin\omega t$, so $A = 3$ and $B = -4$: $c = 3 - 4i$. Amplitude $\abs{c} = 5$, phase $\arg c = -53.13^{\circ}$, so the sum is $5\cos(\omega t - 53.13^{\circ})$.
  4. Why can $e^{i\theta}$ never be zero for real $\theta$?
    Answer
    $\abs{e^{i\theta}}^{2} = e^{i\theta}e^{-i\theta} = e^{0} = 1$, so its length is always one. This is why dividing an equation through by $e^{i\omega t}$ — as section 2.2 does — is always allowed.
  5. What is $\dfrac{\dd}{\dd t}e^{i(\omega t + \varphi)}$, and by what angle does differentiating turn the arrow?
    Answer
    $i\omega\, e^{i(\omega t + \varphi)}$. Multiplying by $i$ is a quarter turn anticlockwise, so the derivative leads the original by $90^{\circ}$ and is $\omega$ times as long.
